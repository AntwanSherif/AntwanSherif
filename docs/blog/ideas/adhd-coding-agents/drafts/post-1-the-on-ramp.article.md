Here's something about how I work that took me years to understand — and longer to stop apologizing for: I don't run at a constant speed. My best work tends to arrive in bursts. Long stretches of steady, unglamorous progress, and then a session where a whole problem clicks into place at once and I ship something I'm genuinely proud of. For a long time I filed that pattern under *lack of discipline.* It turned out to be a cognitive style — one with a name, and a logic I've since learned to build my entire workflow around.

The name is ADHD.

Let me be precise about how I hold that, because the precision matters: I'm self-identified, not formally diagnosed. Like a lot of adults — and, anecdotally, a striking number of engineers — I recognized myself in the description long before I'd ever sit across from a clinician. That's not a cop-out. Adult ADHD is badly under-diagnosed; the best global estimates put it somewhere between roughly 2.5% and 7% of adults depending on how you count, most of them undiagnosed, quietly building their own scaffolding in the dark. I lead with the caveat instead of burying it because I'd rather be straight about the lens than borrow authority I haven't earned — and because the honest version is the useful one for anyone reading this who recognizes themselves in it.

Which is probably why I ended up in software.

Of all the things I could do for a living, code turns out to be unusually kind to a brain like mine. The feedback loop is tight: write something, run it, see it work or fail in seconds. And I didn't just land in software — I gravitated to the front end, where the loop is tighter still. You change a line and *watch* it happen: the layout shifts, the color lands, the thing moves, right there in front of you. It's the difference between being told you got it right and seeing it. For a mind wired to chase what's *interesting* over what's merely important, that's a steady drip of small, vivid wins — each passing test, each bug cornered, each pixel finally sitting where it should. Work that pays back that fast is work I can stay inside.

And when the interest catches, there's <Term id="hyperfocus">hyperfocus</Term> — the same intensity that looks like a "burst" from the outside. From the inside, it's the state where the noise drops away and a whole system holds still in my head at once. It's where I do my most careful work, and where I catch the subtle mistakes I'd otherwise miss. Handled well, it isn't a liability. It's the sharpest tool I own.

Here's one I'm still proud of. I once inherited a front end that took five seconds to show anything — an eternity on the web, the kind of delay where people quietly leave before the app has even said hello. I went deep: the bundle, the render path, what was blocking what. By the end, initial load went from five seconds to four hundred milliseconds.

That didn't come from a spurt of luck. It was weeks of careful, unglamorous work — punctuated by a few hours where the whole dependency graph held still in my head and the bottlenecks lit up like a map. That's the honest division of labor: the burst does the *seeing*; the steady part does the building. And most of the job, every job, is the steady part.

The honest other half is that none of it is on tap. I don't get to schedule hyperfocus, and the same wiring that locks me onto an interesting problem makes the *un*-interesting parts — the boring middle, the finishing twenty percent, the third context-switch of the day — genuinely harder to push through than they are for most people. Left unmanaged, that's exactly where work stalls: at "almost done."

So I don't leave it unmanaged. I've made peace with the tradeoff by taking it seriously instead of wishing it away. External structure — written plans, checklists, deadlines I can't renegotiate with, teammates who keep me honest — isn't decoration for me; it's the scaffolding that turns a spiky attention profile into steady, shippable work. The through-line of my whole career has been building systems that carry the load my <Term id="working-memory">working memory</Term> won't.

For years, those systems were low-tech: notebooks, timers, a religiously-kept task list, the plain discipline of writing things down before they evaporated. They worked — until they didn't, and then I'd rebuild them.

Then, about eighteen months ago, the best system I've ever had arrived, and for once I didn't have to build it myself. Coding agents will hold the plan I keep losing, grind through the boring middle I keep stalling on, and never get bored or wander off to something shinier. The first week, it felt like someone had finally shipped a tool shaped like the inside of my head.

It took me a little longer to notice the catch: the same tool built for the way my brain works can, just as easily, take that brain apart. That's [Part 2](./part-2) — the double-edged sword, and what I've learned to do about it.
