Lesson 03 · Knowledge → Honest Citation

The Digital Body Double

The strongest — and shakiest — evidence in your deck, and how to say so out loud.

Mission tie-in: this is the one you'll get ambushed on. The evidence is compelling and the sample is tiny. The skill this lesson builds — citing n=12 without overclaiming or underselling — is what "handling a hostile Q&A" actually looks like in practice.

Last lesson: the mechanism behind externalizing state — working memory, cognitive offloading, a named intervention. This lesson: a different angle on the same agent-as-cognitive-aid idea. One that has a community name, a Cleveland Clinic definition, and a twelve-person VR study that's either your best citation or your biggest vulnerability. Usually both.

Start with a voice from the field:

"It didn't get distracted. It didn't forget. It didn't move on to something more interesting. It just held the thought." — Chris Wright · fiftyfiveandfive.com/resources/ai-for-adhd

That's not a description of working-memory offload. It's describing something else: presence. Someone — or something — being there with you while you work. That's body doubling.

What the evidence supports

Body doubling is doing a task in the presence of another person who is simply there — working on their own thing, not helping or supervising. The passive presence is the mechanism, not the assistance.

The Cleveland Clinic frames it as "external executive functioning" — they liken it to "having an administrative assistant follow you around all day." For ADHD brains, the presence of another person seems to function as an accountability anchor that improves focus, motivation, and sustained engagement.

Verified  A VR study (arXiv 2509.12153) tested an AI body double against a human one with n=12 ADHD participants on a bricklaying task. Human body double: 10.82 bricks/min. AI body double: 11.06 bricks/min. Efficiency difference between human and AI conditions: p=1.000 — statistically identical. Both conditions significantly outperformed working alone.

⚠ The honest-citation skill

Here's the problem: n=12. Twelve people. One study. A VR bricklaying task.

The direct trial evidence for body doubling — even the traditional, human kind — is minimal. A few small studies, surveys, and clinician consensus. Plausible, cheap, safe to try. Not proven at scale.

On stage, a prepared skeptic will ask: "How many people were in that study?" If you say "twelve" while sounding like you're citing settled science, you've lost the room. If you pre-empt it, you've won credibility you didn't need to fight for.

The move is to say it first. Something like:

"A small study — twelve people — found that an AI body double improved focus comparably to a human one. That's suggestive, not settled. But it matches the mechanism, it's cheap to try, and the ADHD community has been reporting this independently for years."

That's the formula: name the n → name the limit → name why it's still worth citing. A skeptic can't ambush you with information you already gave the audience.

The bridge: pairing with an agent ≈ a digital body double. The agent is co-present, task-focused, and patient. It delivers the functional benefit of body doubling — an external presence that anchors attention and initiates momentum — without requiring another human in the room. That's the tactic, grounded.
